**In short:** Divide the Earth's surface by the number of people and the numbers become easy to picture. In a world of 10 billion people, each of us would have about 10,500 m² of habitable land, roughly one hectare (about 12,800 m² today, with 8.2 billion people). Almost half of it is farmland, and about three quarters of that farmland is used for livestock, which provides only a small part of the world's calories. Cities and roads take only about 1.5%. Our diet is the biggest lever we each have on how land is used.

## The Question

The Earth is so large that its figures, millions of square kilometres, mean little to us. This makes decisions about land, whether personal or collective, hard to think about. This article asks: **if we share the planet's land equally, how much does each person get, and what is it used for?**

## The Earth Per Person

The per-person figures below divide each area by **10 billion people**, roughly where the world's population is expected to peak this century. Today, with about 8.2 billion people, each share is about a fifth larger.

## What This Means

- **About one hectare each.** That is roughly a large city block, or one and a half football pitches, to provide all our food, wood, fibre and wild nature.
- **Farming is the big footprint, not cities.** Everything we have built, cities, roads, factories, covers only about 150 m² per person. Agriculture takes about thirty times more.
- **Most farmland feeds animals.** Grazing land and the crops grown for animal feed take about three quarters of farmland. Yet meat, milk and eggs provide less than a fifth of the world's calories and under two fifths of its protein.
- **Every choice moves the balance.** More land for animals means less left for forests and wild nature. Less meat and dairy frees land that can return to nature.

## Why This Perspective Matters

Seeing land per person:

- makes abstract world statistics personal: you can picture "your" hectare and ask what it is used for;
- shows how individual consumption, especially diet, turns into demand for land;
- raises the question of fairness: if everyone has about the same share, some ways of living use far more than their share, and others far less.

## Examples

- **Diet and land:** a large study of food production around the world found that if everyone shifted to a plant-based diet, global farmland could shrink by about three quarters, an area the size of the United States, China, the European Union and Australia combined, while still feeding everyone.
- **Forests coming back:** where farmland is no longer needed, forests can return. Large parts of Europe have more forest today than a century ago, after farming moved to more productive land.

## The Human Side

Land is not only numbers. It is the landscapes we grow up in, the food on our table and the nature we escape to. Feeling connected to that land, and knowing how our choices affect it, can make sustainable choices feel less like a sacrifice and more like care.
