**In short:** Every society has to make shared choices and settle conflicts. Democracy does this by letting people choose and replace those in power, peacefully. But it only works if people can get reliable information and broadly trust each other. Both are under pressure: democracy has weakened in many countries, and the way news is made and spread has changed fast.

## The Question

[What Every Society Must Do](/how-society-works/societal-functions/) lists conflict resolution and adaptation among society's tasks. [Who Keeps Society Running?](/how-society-works/societal-systems/) names the political and media systems that carry them out. This article asks: **how do societies make good shared decisions, and what do they need to keep doing so?**

## What Democracy Does

Democracy is more than voting. It is a package:

- **Free and fair elections**, so people can replace leaders without violence.
- **Rights and the rule of law**, so the majority cannot simply overrule everyone else, and those in power follow the same rules.
- **Checks and balances:** independent courts, a free press, and a parliament that can say no.
- **Open debate**, so mistakes are noticed and corrected.

That last point matters most over time. No system gets everything right, but one that can notice and fix its mistakes tends to do better in the long run. The economist Amartya Sen observed that no major famine has happened in a functioning democracy with a free press, because governments that can be voted out, and criticised in public, cannot ignore mass hunger.

## Why Information Matters

People can only choose well if they share a reasonably accurate picture of what is happening. That picture comes from journalism, science, statistics, schools and conversation. When it breaks down, people argue not only about what to do, but about what is true.

## What Is Weakening Both

- **Democratic decline:** the V-Dem Institute reports that, by its measures, about three in four people in the world now live under autocratic rule, the highest share in decades, and that the level of democracy for the average person has fallen back to where it was in the late 1970s.
- **The news business:** online platforms took much of the advertising that paid for local and national journalism. Many local newspapers closed, leaving "news deserts".
- **Attention-driven feeds:** platforms that show what keeps people scrolling tend to reward outrage and simple stories over careful ones.
- **Falling trust:** trust in news, parliaments and parties is low in many countries, which makes people easier to mislead and harder to bring together.

## Examples

- **Corrections work:** in many countries, independent fact-checkers, public broadcasters and courts still catch false claims and abuses of power, often long before they would otherwise come to light.
- **Taiwan:** after protests in 2014, Taiwan built open online tools where citizens and officials discuss proposals and look for points of agreement. It is often cited as an example of technology used to strengthen democracy rather than weaken it.

## The Human Side

Having a say matters to people in itself, not only for the results. Feeling heard, being treated as an equal and trusting your neighbours are part of a good life. When people feel nobody listens, they withdraw, or turn to those who promise to break the system. ## Open Questions

- How can good journalism be paid for when advertising has moved online?
- How can platforms be held responsible without giving governments power over what people may say?
- Could citizens' assemblies, chosen by lot like a jury, help with hard long-term choices that elections handle badly?
