**In short:** An economic system is how a society makes things, shares them out and uses them. Every economy does four things: production, distribution, consumption and exchange. Pure market economies are efficient and inventive but unequal and unstable; command economies directed by the state have struggled with waste and lack of freedom. Almost every country today runs a mixed economy, combining markets with public services, social protection and rules. What differs, and what elections often decide, is the balance.

## The Question

[What Every Society Must Do](/how-society-works/societal-functions/) lists sharing resources as one of a society's core jobs, and [Who Keeps Society Running?](/how-society-works/societal-systems/) shows who carries it out. This article asks: **how does an economy actually work, and what choices does it involve?**

## Four Things Every Economy Does

- **Production:** how goods and services are made. The inputs are land, work, capital (tools, machines, infrastructure) and, more and more, knowledge and data.
- **Distribution:** how what is produced is shared across the population. This is where questions of fairness, inequality and access sit.
- **Consumption:** how goods and services are used to meet needs and wants. What we consume feeds back into what is produced next.
- **Exchange:** how buyers and sellers find each other and agree on prices. Markets do most of this in modern economies, but governments, communities and households also organise exchange outside markets: public schools, volunteering, a family cooking for each other.

## Three Kinds of Economic System

- **Market economy (capitalism):** prices and private ownership decide what is made and who gets it. Strengths: efficiency, innovation, quick adaptation. Weaknesses: inequality, costs that don't show in prices (such as pollution), and cycles of booms and busts.
- **Command economy:** the state directs production and distribution from the centre. It has historically struggled with waste, with not having the information it needs, and with a lack of personal freedom. The Soviet Union's shortages were a well-known result.
- **Mixed economy:** markets combined with government action for public goods, social protection and regulation. Almost every modern country works this way. In most rich countries, public spending is between about a third and over half of the economy.

## Five Ideas Every Citizen Should Know

- **GDP (gross domestic product):** the total value of what a country produces in a year. Widely used but imperfect: it measures activity, not well-being. Repairing damage after a flood adds to GDP; unpaid care at home does not.
- **Inflation:** how fast prices rise. A little is normal (central banks usually aim for about 2% a year). High or unpredictable inflation eats into savings and wages, as many people felt in 2022, when inflation in the euro area peaked at over 10%.
- **Monetary policy:** what central banks do with interest rates and the money supply to keep prices and the economy steady.
- **Fiscal policy:** what governments do with taxes and spending.
- **Inequality:** how income and wealth are spread across people. High inequality has been linked to lower social mobility, worse health and political instability.

## Examples

- **Post-war Western Europe:** after 1945, mixed economies combined markets with strong public services and social insurance, and grew fast for three decades while inequality fell.
- **China since 1978:** a gradual opening to markets inside a state-led system lifted hundreds of millions of people out of extreme poverty, while inequality rose.

## The Human Side

The economy can feel like something distant, run by experts. But it shapes daily life directly: the price of rent and food, whether work is secure, whether there is money left at the end of the month. Understanding the basics helps people make better personal decisions and feel less powerless in public debates. ## Open Questions

- What should be left to markets, and what should be public, such as health care, housing or energy?
- How should we measure whether an economy is doing well, if GDP misses so much?
- Can economies keep growing within the planet's limits, or must they change what they grow?
