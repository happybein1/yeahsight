## A Natural Process With Side Effects

Inheritance is how a society carries itself forward. Each generation passes on what it has built: knowledge, values, habits, networks, and possessions. Without it, every generation would start from nothing.

But inheritance also carries inequities forward. When some people start life with far more than others, the gap tends to persist and grow. That can slow development for everyone, because it breeds insecurity, resentment, and a sense that effort matters less than origin.

## Two Kinds of Inheritance

It helps to separate two forms:

- **Social inheritance**: education, language, habits, confidence, and the networks a family gives its children. It shapes opportunity strongly, but it cannot be handed over as a lump sum.
- **Financial inheritance**: money, property, and assets passed on at death, or as gifts and donations during life. It can be transferred in full, and it can be very large.

The two call for different responses.

## Smoothing Social Inheritance

Differences in social inheritance can be reduced over time through good general education, open access to knowledge, and support for every person's basic goals and needs. This is slow, but it works with how people actually grow, and it doesn't take anything away from anyone.

## Why Financial Inheritance Is Harder

Financial inheritance concentrates resources, and with them power, among a small number of people who did not earn them. Because capital generates more capital, these advantages compound across generations.

Education alone can't close that gap. It needs deeper changes in the system, especially in areas where development requires large investments up front, such as housing, starting a business, or acquiring land.

## HappyBein's Proposed Direction

*This is a proposal for discussion, not a finished policy. Some points still need further clarification and support.*

The proposal is to **cap what each person can receive** in their lifetime, through inheritance and gifts together, and to **give every young adult a fair starting capital**. In practice, this could mean:

- Each person can receive up to a set amount over their whole life, for example a few hundred thousand euros. Below the cap, nothing changes: parents can still pass on something meaningful.
- Anything above the cap goes to a common fund rather than to the heir.
- That fund finances what every person needs to start and live well: a **starting capital** for each young adult, for education, a first home or a business, alongside the [Universal Allowance](/sharing-fairly/universal-allowance) for basic needs.
- Because the cap applies to each person who receives, someone can leave something to many people (children, nieces and nephews, friends, godchildren), each within the cap. This also gives people without children a fair way to pass on what they built, and it spreads wealth widely instead of concentrating it.
- Gifts to charities and other public-benefit organisations stay allowed, so people can keep supporting the causes they care about.
- Personal belongings with sentimental value (photos, letters, family objects) remain within families.
- Large gifts during life count towards the same cap, so the rule can't be avoided by giving early.

The goal is not to punish success, but to make sure each generation starts from a fairer base, and that wealth keeps circulating instead of concentrating.

## Two Common Objections

**"People will lose the motivation to work and build."** Most people work for their own life, their security, their status and the meaning they find in it, much more than for what they leave behind. All of that stays: what people earn and spend during their life is theirs. Research on large inheritances even suggests the opposite effect on heirs: people who inherit a lot tend to work less (Holtz-Eakin, Joulfaian and Rosen, 1993).

**"Without large family fortunes, who will invest?"** Much investment already comes from pension funds and people's savings, and a public investment fund can add to it. Norway's sovereign fund shows such a fund can be large, long-term and transparently managed. Family businesses and farms keep a path to pass on the business itself (see below), so working enterprises aren't broken up.

## Points to Work Through

A change of this size raises real questions:

- **Family attachment**: many people work hard precisely to secure their children's future. The cap leaves room for that, and a fair starting capital for everyone adds a new kind of security rather than just removing one.
- **Family businesses and farms**: these need a transition path so that working enterprises aren't broken up, for example paying what is due over many years out of the business's profits.
- **Avoidance**: moving assets abroad or into companies would need to be addressed, ideally through international coordination, for example at EU level.
- **Trust in public institutions**: redistribution only works if people trust that the fund is managed transparently and fairly.

The cap can start generous and come down gradually on a published schedule, so people have time to plan. [Making the Transition](/sharing-fairly/making-the-transition/) describes the order and pace.

## The Human Side

Inheritance touches some of our deepest drivers: security, belonging, and the wish to care for those who come after us. Any change should respect these needs while meeting them more fairly for everyone.
