## A Natural Process With Side Effects

Inheritance is how a society carries itself forward. Each generation passes on what it has built: knowledge, values, habits, networks, and possessions. Without it, every generation would start from nothing.

But inheritance also carries inequities forward. When some people start life with far more than others, the gap tends to persist and grow. That can slow development for everyone, because it breeds insecurity, resentment, and a sense that effort matters less than origin.

## Two Kinds of Inheritance

It helps to separate two forms:

- **Social inheritance**: education, language, habits, confidence, and the networks a family gives its children. It shapes opportunity strongly, but it cannot be handed over as a lump sum.
- **Financial inheritance**: money, property, and assets passed on at death, or as gifts and donations during life. It can be transferred in full, and it can be very large.

The two call for different responses.

## Smoothing Social Inheritance

Differences in social inheritance can be reduced over time through good general education, open access to knowledge, and support for every person's basic goals and needs. This is slow, but it works with how people actually grow, and it doesn't take anything away from anyone.

## Why Financial Inheritance Is Harder

Financial inheritance concentrates resources, and with them power, among a small number of people who did not earn them. Because capital generates more capital, these advantages compound across generations.

Education alone can't close that gap. It needs deeper changes in the system, especially in areas where development requires large investments up front, such as housing, starting a business, or acquiring land.

## HappyBein's Proposed Direction

*This is a proposal for discussion, not a finished policy. Some points still need further clarification and support.*

One option is to **phase out financial inheritance and large donations**, and instead use the public system to redistribute accumulated assets fairly among people. In practice, this could mean:

- When a person dies, their financial assets return to a common fund rather than to selected heirs.
- That fund finances what every person needs to start and live well: the [Universal Allowance](/sharing-fairly/universal-allowance) for basic needs, education, and a fair starting capital for each young adult.
- Personal belongings with sentimental value (photos, letters, family objects) remain within families.
- Large gifts during life are treated the same way, so the rule can't be avoided by giving early.

The goal is not to punish success, but to make sure each generation starts from a fairer base, and that wealth keeps circulating instead of concentrating.

## Points to Work Through

A change of this size raises real questions:

- **Family attachment**: many people work hard precisely to secure their children's future. A fair starting capital for everyone would need to replace that sense of security, not just remove it.
- **Family businesses and farms**: these may need a transition path so that working enterprises aren't broken up.
- **Avoidance**: moving assets abroad or into companies would need to be addressed, ideally through international coordination.
- **Trust in public institutions**: redistribution only works if people trust that the fund is managed transparently and fairly.

Intermediate steps, such as higher taxes on very large inheritances or a lifetime cap on what one person can receive, can move in this direction while these questions are worked out.

## The Human Side

Inheritance touches some of our deepest drivers: security, belonging, and the wish to care for those who come after us. Any change should respect these needs while meeting them more fairly for everyone.
