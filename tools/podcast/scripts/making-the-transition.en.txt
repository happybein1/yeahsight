# Making the Transition

This is YeahSight, by HappyBein. In this episode: making the transition. How a society could move away from inherited financial unfairness, towards a society that is fair and sustainable. Step by step, without breaking what works, and without leaving people behind.

A quick note before we start. This is a proposal, not a finished policy. Some of these ideas still need more work. They are here to be discussed, and improved.

## In short

Most of today's financial unfairness was inherited, not chosen. Wealth keeps earning more wealth, and it passes from one generation to the next.

Fixing this in one go would cause fear, resistance, and damage. So we propose a slow path, announced well in advance, in three phases. First, give everyone a secure floor. Then, change how new wealth flows: inheritances, rising land values, and pollution. Finally, let the large inherited fortunes return to a common fund, that pays for everyone's start in life.

Ordinary savings, pensions, and family homes are protected all along the way. Any country can start the first steps on its own. The parts where money can move with one click are done by a group of countries together. And every step is chosen to make the economy stronger, not weaker, so that going first doesn't mean falling behind.

## The question

Other YeahSight articles describe where we want to go. Basic needs guaranteed for everyone. Each generation starting from a fairer base. And the planet's resources shared within its limits.

This episode asks the harder question. How do we get there from here? Starting from a world where the richest ten percent of people own about three quarters of all wealth, and the poorer half owns about two percent.

## Why change is hard

Plans for a fairer society usually fail on the way there, not at the destination. Four things make the transition difficult.

First, unfairness feeds itself. Wealth invested in shares, property, or land tends to grow faster than wages. Those who already have it get further ahead every year, without doing anything. And their children start ahead too.

Second, people fear losing what they have. A family that owns its home, or has saved for retirement, can easily feel threatened by any talk of redistribution. Even when it is aimed at fortunes a thousand times larger.

Third, countries compete. If one country acts alone, large fortunes, companies, and investment can move to another one. Even a large group feels the pressure. The European Union, which has gone further than most on fairness and climate, is falling behind the United States and China in income growth.

And fourth, governments change. A reform that one government passes, the next one can cancel. Long changes need support that lasts beyond one election.

A good transition plan has to answer all four.

## What history teaches

Large, peaceful shifts in who owns what have happened before.

West Germany, in 1952. After the war, a law called the "equalisation of burdens" asked people who had kept their property to pay a levy of up to half of its value. But they could pay it in small instalments, over thirty years, mostly out of their income. The money went to the millions who had lost everything. Spread out like that, it became bearable. And it helped hold the country together.

Japan, from 1946 to 1950. A land reform bought farmland from large landowners, and sold it cheaply to the families who worked it. It worked: rural poverty fell sharply. But the landowners were paid in bonds that inflation soon made almost worthless, so many of them felt it as confiscation. The lesson: fast and abrupt can work, but it leaves bitterness.

Alaska, and Norway. Both turned their oil income into a common fund, instead of letting it go to a few. Alaska pays every resident a yearly dividend from its fund, and has done so since 1982. Norway saves its oil money in one of the largest funds in the world, and only spends about the yearly return. Shared funds like these have lasted for decades, under governments of every colour, because everyone can see what they get from them.

Finland, in 2017 and 2018. A two-year test gave two thousand unemployed people five hundred and sixty euros a month, with no conditions. They worked slightly more, not less. And they felt much more secure, and more trusting. Testing small first gave real evidence, instead of guesses.

The global minimum tax, in 2021. More than a hundred and thirty countries agreed that large companies should pay at least fifteen percent tax, wherever they operate. Shared rules like this one show that "the money will just leave" can be answered, when countries act together.

And Ireland, from 2016 to 2018. A citizens' assembly of ninety-nine people, chosen at random, studied hard questions for months. Its advice on abortion led to the 2018 referendum. When ordinary people are given the time and the facts, they can find agreement, even on deeply divisive subjects.

## Our proposal: eight principles

One. Floor first. Secure everyone's basic needs, before asking anyone to give anything up. People accept change much more easily when they are not afraid.

Two. Announce everything early, including the rules for leaving. Publish the whole path and its dates many years in advance, so that nobody's plans are destroyed overnight. But the rules for those who leave the country apply from the very day of the announcement. Slow for those who stay. No head start for those who leave.

Three. Protect the ordinary, and focus on the concentrated. Family homes up to a fair value, pensions, normal savings, and working family businesses are protected. The changes apply to very large fortunes.

Four. Make the economy stronger, not weaker. A fairness plan that weakens the economy won't last. Steps that are fair and also raise productivity come first. And the pace follows the economic strength of the countries taking part.

Five. Change the flows before the stocks. Start with new money: inheritances, land values that rise without any effort from the owner, pollution, and resource use. Only later, and gradually, touch the fortunes that already exist.

Six. Make the gains visible. The money raised goes into a common fund that people can see. It pays for a starting capital for every young adult, and for the basic-needs allowance. Not into the general budget, where nobody sees what it pays for.

Seven. Tax first what can't move, and do the rest as a group. Land, buildings, and natural resources stay where they are, whoever owns them. So any country can start there, on its own. Money that can move with one click is taxed by a group of countries large enough to matter, protected by shared rules at its borders.

Eight. Decide together, and test first. Use citizens' assemblies to shape the details, and pilots to check that each step works, before it goes national.

## Alone, or together?

A simple rule decides who does what. If it stays put, one country can act alone. If it can move with one click, countries act together.

One country can do all of this alone. Guarantee basic needs, with pilots first, then the full allowance. Public registers of who owns land, buildings, and companies. Citizens' assemblies on the plan. Taxing land and buildings more, and work less. Taxing rent and profits from local land, buildings, and companies, where they are. Rules for those who leave the country. A fossil-fuel tax on homes, transport, and local businesses, with the money returned to people. And starting the common fund, and the starting capital for young adults.

A group of countries is needed for the rest. Taxing financial wealth held anywhere: shares, funds, and bank accounts abroad. The lifetime cap on large inheritances. A fossil-fuel tax on industries that compete with other countries, with a border charge on imports. Minimum rates that nobody goes below, so that no member takes fortunes from another. And a small shared fund.

Phase one is everything a country can do alone, so any country can start tomorrow. Phase two begins once a group is ready. And if no group forms yet, a country doesn't stop. It goes further on what it can do alone, and keeps pushing for the group.

## Three phases

Phase one: build the floor, and the trust. That's roughly the first five years.

Pilot a basic-needs allowance in a few regions, then extend it step by step. Make ownership visible, with a public register of who really owns companies, land, and large fortunes. Tax the wealth income that can't escape, where it is earned: rent from buildings, gains on land, and the profits of companies based in the country. And start shifting taxes off work, and onto land.

Put the rules for leaving in place, from the day the plan is announced. From that same day, gifts also count towards the future lifetime cap. So giving everything away early doesn't escape it.

Start a common fund, at first from taxes on land and rent. Tax fossil fuels and pollution used by homes, transport, and local businesses, and give all of that money back to people, equally, through the fund. Most ordinary households get back more than they pay, because the rich use far more energy. That way, fairness and sustainability are paid for by the same move. And hold a citizens' assembly on the full path, and publish its timeline.

Phase two: change the flows, together. Roughly years five to twenty.

With a group of countries, tax income from all financial wealth, wherever it is held, at the same rates as income from work, and close the main loopholes. Extend the fossil-fuel tax to industries that compete abroad, with a border charge, so that imports pay the same.

Introduce a lifetime cap on what one person can receive in inheritances and gifts. Anything above the cap goes to the common fund. The cap starts generous, and is lowered on a published schedule. It applies to heirs who live in the group of countries, wherever the person giving lives.

Keep shifting taxes away from work, and onto land values and resource use, which nobody created by their own effort. The fund starts paying a starting capital to every young adult, for example at eighteen or twenty-five, for education, a first home, or a business. And family businesses and farms get a path to pass on the business itself, for example by paying over many years out of its profits, like the German levy.

Phase three: complete the shift. Roughly years twenty to forty.

Financial inheritance beyond the cap, and large donations, are phased out. Personal and sentimental belongings stay in families. The common fund, now large, pays for the basic-needs allowance and everyone's starting capital. And what a person owns then reflects mainly their own life and work, no longer the luck of being born into a rich family.

## What if one country goes first?

Competition between countries is real. It is the main reason fair plans get watered down. But the answer is not to wait until every country agrees. And it is certainly not to abolish countries. What's needed is a shared floor of rules, with each country free to go further. That is already how trade, the protection of the ozone layer, and the global minimum tax work.

Five things make it possible to go first without losing.

One. Tax what can't leave first. Land, buildings, and natural resources stay in the country, whoever owns them. That is why the plan starts there.

Two. Put the rules for leaving first, from day one. Announcing the plan without them would invite fortunes to leave before it even starts. So, from the day of the announcement, people who move away pay tax on the gains they built up here, as if they had sold. Germany, France, and Norway already do this. The rules follow them for ten years. Germany already applies its inheritance tax to Germans for five years after they move abroad, and the United States taxes its citizens wherever they live. The lifetime cap counts where the heirs live, so a rich parent moving abroad doesn't help if the children stay. And a small independent body reviews the rules regularly, and closes new loopholes, without changing the path itself.

None of this forbids leaving. And it takes nothing from those who stay. Inside the European Union, a tax on leaving for another member country can only be collected when the assets are actually sold. One more reason why the group must share the same rules.

Three. Protect the borders, without closing them. A high fossil-fuel tax alone would hurt factories that sell abroad. So for them, it only comes with a border charge. From 2026, the European Union charges imports from countries without a carbon price the difference, so producers who go first aren't undercut. The same idea can apply to other shared rules.

Four. Start as a group, not alone. A group the size of the European Union is too large a market to walk away from. Others then join over time, as happened with the global minimum tax.

Five. Choose steps that make the economy stronger. Europe's slower growth comes mainly from expensive energy, too little investment in new technology, and markets split into twenty-seven sets of rules. Not from fairness itself. Several steps in this plan address those weaknesses directly. Moving taxes off work and onto land makes work cheaper for businesses. Cheap renewable energy goes straight at Europe's biggest cost problem. A starting capital for every young adult means more people able to study, start a business, or take a risk. And the common fund can invest, as Norway's does, with a small, capped part for new technology at home.

Countries also gain from fairness in ways growth figures don't show. Denmark, Sweden, and the Netherlands combine high taxes with strong economies, partly because security, good education, and trust attract people and businesses too.

## Who manages the fund?

The common fund will become very large. So it has to be protected from waste, and from political favours. No single party should control it. The job is split four ways.

One. Parliament writes the rules once, in a law that is hard to change. The law sets what the fund pays for, how much it may pay out each year, and what it may invest in. It doesn't decide, year by year, where the money goes.

Two. An independent public body manages the money day to day. Professional managers with long, fixed terms invest it at arm's length from the government, the way a central bank sets interest rates. Norway's oil fund is run this way, by a unit of its central bank, not by ministers.

Three. Citizens watch over it. A small panel of citizens chosen at random, renewed regularly, reviews the fund every year, and reports publicly. The public auditor checks the accounts. And anyone can see online what came in, and what went out.

Four. Payouts follow a fixed formula, not a yearly political choice. For example, the fund pays out at most about three percent of its value each year, shared equally. Norway uses a similar rule.

Both the fund and its payout formula need the strongest protection available, such as the constitution, or a large majority, or a referendum, to change them. Alaska shows why. Its fund is protected in the state constitution, but the yearly dividend formula was only an ordinary law. Since 2016, short of money, the state has repeatedly paid out less than the formula promised. And trust has suffered.

Most of the fund stays in broad, low-risk investments, spread across many companies and countries. Investing at home is where political favours creep in: "put the money into my region's factory". That's why Norway's fund invests only abroad. So only a small, capped part goes to new technology at home, under its own published rules.

## Is the fund shared between countries?

Not at first. Each country keeps its own fund, filled by its own taxes, and answering to its own parliament and citizens. People accept paying more when they can see it coming back to them and their neighbours. And countries start at very different levels of wealth. A single fund from day one would mean large transfers between them: the fastest way to lose voters on both sides.

What the group shares from the start are the rules. The same inheritance cap rules, the same ownership registers, and the same payout principles. That way, nobody gains by moving a fortune from one member country to another.

On top of that, a small shared fund handles what crosses borders. It is paid for by money the group raises together, mainly the carbon border charge. It helps poorer members reach the common floor faster, and pays for shared investments, such as power grids between countries. There is a precedent. In 2020, the European Union borrowed together, at large scale, for the first time: about seven hundred and fifty billion euros, for the recovery after Covid.

As trust grows, and the gaps between countries shrink, more can be pooled. People who move between member countries keep their rights. The allowance follows where you live, and your starting capital is yours wherever you go.

## Objections

"It's my money, and I want to give it to my children." That wish is deeply human. That's why the plan starts by guaranteeing every child a floor, and a starting capital. The security parents want for their own children becomes something all children get.

"The rich will leave." Some will try. The answer is a group of countries acting together, taxes on leaving, ownership registers, and taxes tied to land and activity inside the country, which can't be moved abroad.

"We'll lose against countries that don't do this." Only if the steps make the economy weaker. That's why the steps that also raise productivity come first.

"Governments will waste the money." That's why the fund is separate, transparent, with clear rules on what it pays for, and visible to every citizen.

"The next government will cancel it." A path decided with citizens, announced long in advance, and paying benefits everyone can see, is much harder to cancel. Few politicians would dare to take away every young adult's starting capital.

"Just announcing it will make them leave." That's why the rules for leaving apply from the day of the announcement, and gifts count towards the cap from that day too. The slow part is what people who stay will pay. Not the protection against escaping it.

"A fossil-fuel tax hurts competitiveness." For industries that compete abroad, it would. So they are only taxed with a border charge on imports. For homes, transport, and local businesses, all the money goes back to people. Sweden has had one of the world's highest carbon taxes since 1991. Its emissions fell, while its economy kept growing.

"Forty years is too slow." Slow at the top, but not at the bottom. The floor comes first, in the first years. The slowness is only in the part that, done fast, would cause fear and flight.

## How it could actually happen

This is not a programme for one candidate who gets elected and does it all. Even a president needs a majority in parliament. The plan runs over about ten elections. And the parts done together need several governments to agree at the same time. It can't belong to one person, or one party.

Big changes usually follow the same pattern, over ten to thirty years. First, an idea is written down clearly. In 1942, the British Beveridge Report described a welfare state. Second, people and organisations spread it and push for it: associations, unions, researchers, and journalists. Third, parties take up pieces of it, often several parties, each with the parts that suit them. Fourth, a crisis or an election opens a window, and a piece becomes law. After the war, Britain built its welfare state from the Beveridge Report within a few years. And fifth, once people see the benefits, it becomes hard to undo. Few politicians today would dare to remove public health care.

Some pieces of this plan are already on that path. The global minimum tax came after years of pressure from researchers and campaign groups. In 2024, Brazil asked the G20 to study a minimum tax on billionaires, based on a report by the economist Gabriel Zucman. And Ireland's citizens' assemblies began as a small trial by university researchers in 2011, before the government took up the idea.

So this plan is best seen as a menu: pieces that different movements, parties, and countries can pick up over time. What it adds is the overall picture. How the pieces fit together, in what order, and why each one is safe to do. And YeahSight's part is the first step: explaining the idea clearly, so that others can spread it, and use it.

## First steps

Some things can start now, without waiting for the whole plan. Join or support a group already working on a piece of it. You'll find a directory of them on YeahSight. Support public registers of who really owns companies and land. Ask candidates, in every election, where they stand on two or three concrete pieces of this plan. In Europe, use the European Citizens' Initiative: with one million signatures from at least seven countries, the European Commission must formally respond. Support taxing income from wealth and income from work in the same way. Back pilots of guaranteed basic needs where you live. Ask for citizens' assemblies on wealth and inheritance. And in your own life, talk about what you will pass on, and why.

## The human side

Money is rarely just money. For most people, it stands for safety, freedom, and the ability to look after those they love. A fair transition shouldn't take these away from anyone. It should make them reliable for everyone, so that nobody has to hoard to feel safe.

## Open questions

Some questions remain open. Where exactly should the lifetime cap start, and how fast should it fall? What counts as a fair-value family home? How can countries at different levels of wealth move along this path together? And how can the past be acknowledged, without making the transition impossible?
