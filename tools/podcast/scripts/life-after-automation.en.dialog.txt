S: The other side of the change. For most of history, work has organised our lives. It gives income, but also a schedule, an identity, colleagues, and a feeling of contributing. So what happens when machines do most of it?
E: Let's start with the near future. How does our relationship with AI change?
S: At first, AI makes something and a person checks and approves it. A partnership. As systems get more reliable, it's tempting to hand over more. So the likely path is from doing to overseeing: people spend less time on the work itself and more time checking it, catching errors, and staying ready to step in.
E: Can rules keep people in control?
S: In principle. But making those rules hold across companies, industries and countries is a hard coordination problem, and there's no guarantee it will work everywhere.
E: The article has an interesting idea about backup skills.
S: Yes. Overseeing doesn't mean we can forget how the work is done. If a system fails after years of running a task, through an outage, an attack, a bad update or a strange case, someone must be able to take over. So we need what the article calls AI backup training: people who really know how to fly the plane, treat the patient or run the system by hand, even if they rarely need to.
E: Otherwise?
S: Otherwise skills quietly fade, until the day they're needed most. Treating core skills as unnecessary once AI takes over trades a small saving now for a much bigger risk later.
E: Now jump ahead. Most work is automated. What then?
S: Then the question is no longer which jobs disappear, but what fills the space work used to fill. And even if income no longer depends on work, through lower costs or redistribution, many people face a question money can't answer: what do I do with my time, and why does it matter?
E: Because work gave us more than money.
S: Exactly. A sense of being needed. Structure and routine. People around us with shared goals. And mastery, the satisfaction of getting better at something hard. Take that away suddenly and at scale, and many people could have plenty of time but no direction. Better technology doesn't solve that. If anything, the better AI serves us, the sharper the question gets.
E: So what stays human?
S: Things that keep their value because a person does them. Relationships and community: most of us don't want those automated, even if an AI could imitate them. Creativity and entertainment where part of the value is knowing a person made it. Competition, like sport and strategy games, where human limits are part of the point. And craftsmanship: making things yourself, even when a machine could do it faster.
E: Working for pleasure, not need.
S: Yes. People already cook from scratch, restore furniture or grow vegetables for the joy of it. When survival no longer depends on work, that kind of voluntary work could become a main source of structure and satisfaction.
E: And there's an energy angle.
S: Automation at scale, data centres, robots, AI, uses a lot of electricity. Low-tech, hands-on activities like gardening, cooking, repairing, walking or cycling, crafts and music, are rewarding in themselves, and a small counterweight to that demand. Not a sacrifice. Just a legitimate way to spend time, rather than an inefficiency to automate away.
E: Is there an upside to all this?
S: Possibly a big one. If AI also helps with the political and coordination problems that fuel conflict over scarce resources, we could imagine less large-scale conflict, and more capacity for shared challenges like sustainability. But that depends on choices and governance. It isn't automatic.
E: What can a listener do now?
S: Ask how much of your sense of purpose comes from what you produce, and how much from who you are and how you connect with others. The second part is much harder to automate. Invest in activities that give real meaning. If you oversee an automated process, keep practising the skill underneath. And start thinking now about where you'd find meaning if paid work weren't the centre of your time. This may come sooner than expected.
