E: Machines replacing work isn't new. What's different now?
S: The speed, the range, and the intelligence. Machines used to take over physical tasks. Now they also do mental ones: reading documents, writing code, reading medical scans, finding their way through complex places.
E: Which tasks go first?
S: Traditionally, the routine, predictable, well-defined ones, physical or mental. Assembly lines, data entry, standard analysis. What's harder to automate is real creativity, work that needs a lot of emotional intelligence, like care or difficult negotiations, judgment in unclear, high-stakes situations, and physical work in messy, unpredictable places.
E: But that line is moving.
S: Fast, especially with AI. Knowledge work built on pulling information together, like consulting, finance or diagnostics, is exposed earlier than that old pattern suggests. Because AI is becoming better at parts of it, not just cheaper or faster.
E: What does that mean for jobs?
S: A large share of today's jobs contain tasks that can be automated. That doesn't mean the jobs vanish, but many will change a lot. Office and service work is usually hit first, which can mean less demand for some roles and pressure on wages. Physical work follows more slowly, because robots spread more slowly. But once robotics is ready for a given task, jobs in transport, manufacturing or skilled trades could shift within roughly five to ten years. That's a much shorter time to adjust than past changes allowed.
E: Doesn't automation also create new jobs?
S: Historically, about as many as it removed. But with a delay, and the new jobs need different skills. The cost of that gap tends to fall on the workers with the fewest alternatives.
E: There's also an effect on prices.
S: Yes, and it's often overlooked. When machines produce goods and services more cheaply, prices tend to fall. Food, manufactured goods, transport, some services. So you might need less income for the same standard of living.
E: That sounds like good news.
S: Partly. It depends which costs fall. Housing and health care weigh much more on living costs than electronics, and they've resisted automation's price drops so far. So cheaper goods alone won't make up for less paid work.
E: So how might work end up being shared?
S: I see three forces, running side by side. First, concentration. Employers tend to prefer fewer workers who are good with automation, rather than spreading work over more people. Left alone, you get a smaller group working full time, while others compete for fewer jobs at lower wages.
E: Second?
S: Choosing to work less. If living costs fall enough, some people may simply decide to work fewer hours, because they need less. Not everyone, but those with some cushion.
E: And third?
S: Policy stepping in. If unemployment becomes too much for society to bear, history suggests governments react, usually late rather than early: shorter working weeks, more redistribution, or rewards for sharing jobs.
E: What can a person do now?
S: Build skills that are deeply human: communication, empathy, creativity, complex reasoning. Learn to use AI tools as a multiplier instead of fighting them. Keep learning, because specific skills go out of date faster. And build some financial resilience, like an emergency fund, for the bumpy period between old and new work.
E: And organisations?
S: Invest in retraining alongside automation. Redesign jobs around what people do well. Be open with staff about automation plans. And think about the wider effect on society.
E: And governments?
S: Education that teaches people to adapt, not just fixed content. A safety net strong enough for people in transition. And tax systems that may need to change, if more value goes to machines and less comes from taxing work. If that's not enough, mechanisms like a basic income come into play, and choices about which roles we keep human on purpose, because the human part is what gives them value.
