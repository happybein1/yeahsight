E: Welcome to YeahSight, from HappyBein, where we look at how society works and where it's heading. I'm Emma, and today Sam is here to talk about making the transition. Hi Sam.
S: Hi Emma, good to be here.
E: This one is a proposal, not just an explanation. What's the question?
S: Other articles describe where we'd like to go: basic needs guaranteed for everyone, a fairer start for each generation, and the planet shared within its limits. This one asks the harder question: how do we get there from here?
E: And where is "here"?
S: A world where the richest ten percent own about three quarters of all wealth, and the poorer half owns about two percent.
E: So why not just fix it?
S: Because plans like this usually fail on the way, not at the destination. Four things make it hard. First, unfairness feeds itself: wealth in shares, property or land tends to grow faster than wages, so those who have it get further ahead every year, and so do their children.
E: Second?
S: People fear losing what they have. A family with a home or retirement savings can feel threatened by any talk of redistribution, even when it's aimed at fortunes a thousand times larger.
E: That's very human.
S: It is. Third, countries compete. If one country acts alone, fortunes and companies can move elsewhere. And fourth, governments change. What one passes, the next can cancel.
E: Has anything like this ever worked?
S: Several times, peacefully. After the war, West Germany asked people who had kept their property to pay a levy of up to half its value. But they could pay it in small amounts over thirty years, mostly out of income. The money went to the millions who had lost everything, and spread out like that, it was bearable.
E: Thirty years. That's patient.
S: Very. Compare Japan, just after the war. A land reform moved farmland from big landowners to the families who worked it. Rural poverty fell sharply. But landowners were paid in bonds that inflation made almost worthless, so many felt robbed. Fast works, but it leaves bitterness.
E: Any examples with money people can actually see?
S: Alaska and Norway. Both turned oil income into a common fund. Alaska pays every resident a yearly dividend, and Norway saves its oil money and only spends about the yearly return. Those funds have lasted for decades, under every kind of government, because everyone sees what they get.
E: So what does HappyBein propose?
S: A slow, announced path in three phases, built on a few principles. The first is: floor first. Secure everyone's basic needs before asking anyone to give anything up. People accept change much more easily when they're not afraid.
E: What else?
S: Announce everything early, many years in advance, so nobody's plans are destroyed overnight. Protect the ordinary: family homes, pensions, normal savings and working family businesses. The changes are aimed at very large fortunes. And change the flows before the stocks.
E: Meaning?
S: Start with new money: inheritances, land that gains value without any effort from its owner, and pollution. Only later, and gradually, touch fortunes that already exist.
E: And where does the money go?
S: Into a common fund people can see. It pays for the basic-needs allowance and a starting capital for every young adult, for education, a first home or a business. Not into the general budget, where nobody sees what it pays for.
E: What about the problem of fortunes simply leaving?
S: There's a simple rule. If something stays put, like land, buildings or natural resources, one country can tax it alone. If money can move with one click, a group of countries acts together.
E: So walk me through the three phases.
S: Phase one, roughly the first five years. Pilot the allowance in a few regions, then extend it. Publish registers of who really owns land, buildings and companies. Tax rent and land gains, and shift taxes off work. Start the common fund. And put a tax on fossil fuels for homes and transport, with all of it given back to people equally.
E: All of it?
S: All of it. Most ordinary households get back more than they pay, because the rich use far more energy. Fairness and the climate are paid for by the same move.
E: Then phase two.
S: Roughly years five to twenty, with a group of countries. Income from wealth is taxed like income from work, wherever it's held. And a lifetime cap comes in on what one person can receive in inheritances and gifts. Anything above it goes to the common fund. The cap starts generous and is lowered on a published schedule.
E: And family farms or businesses?
S: They get a path to pass on the business itself, for example paying over many years out of its profits, like the German levy.
E: And phase three?
S: Years twenty to forty. The cap reaches its final level. People can still inherit a meaningful amount, and personal belongings stay in families. The fund, now large, pays for everyone's allowance and starting capital. What you own then reflects mainly your own life and work, not the luck of being born into a rich family.
E: Let's come back to competition. What if one country goes first?
S: It can, without losing. It starts with what can't leave. And from the very day the plan is announced, there are rules for leaving: people who move away pay tax on the gains they built up here, and the rules follow them for ten years. Germany, France and Norway already do versions of this.
E: And heirs?
S: The cap counts where the heirs live. A rich parent moving abroad doesn't help if the children stay. None of this forbids leaving. It just removes the head start.
E: Won't it hurt the economy?
S: Only if the steps make it weaker, so the ones that make it stronger come first. Moving taxes off work and onto land makes work cheaper for businesses, and economists widely see land tax as the least harmful tax there is. Cheap renewable energy tackles one of Europe's biggest costs. And a starting capital means more young people can study, start a business or take a risk.
E: Who would manage this huge fund? That sounds tempting for politicians.
S: Exactly why no single party should control it. Parliament writes the rules once, in a law that's hard to change. An independent public body manages the money, like Norway's fund, which is run by a unit of its central bank. A panel of citizens chosen at random reviews it every year. And payouts follow a fixed formula, say about three percent of the fund each year, shared equally.
E: Why does the formula need protecting?
S: Look at Alaska. Its fund is in the state constitution, but the dividend formula was only an ordinary law. Since 2016, short of money, the state has repeatedly paid out less than promised, and trust has suffered.
E: Let me try a few objections. "It's my money, and I want to give it to my children."
S: That wish is deeply human. That's why the plan starts by guaranteeing every child a floor and a starting capital. The security parents want for their own children becomes something all children get.
E: "The next government will cancel it."
S: A path decided with citizens, announced long in advance, with benefits everyone can see, is much harder to cancel. Few politicians would dare take away every young adult's starting capital.
E: "Forty years is far too slow."
S: Slow at the top, not at the bottom. The floor comes first, in the first few years. The slow part is the one that, done fast, would cause fear and flight.
E: So who actually does all this? One great leader?
S: No. The plan runs over about ten elections, and several governments have to agree. Big changes usually follow a pattern. An idea is written down clearly, like Britain's Beveridge Report in 1942, which described a welfare state. People and organisations spread it. Parties take up pieces. Then a crisis or an election opens a window, and a piece becomes law. Once people see the benefits, it's hard to undo.
E: And some pieces are already moving?
S: Yes. More than a hundred and thirty countries agreed a global minimum tax on large companies in 2021. In 2024, Brazil asked the G20 to study a minimum tax on billionaires. So think of this plan as a menu, pieces that different movements, parties and countries can pick up over time.
E: What can a listener do today?
S: Support a group already working on a piece of it; YeahSight has a directory of them. Ask candidates where they stand on two or three concrete steps. Back pilots of guaranteed basic needs, or citizens' assemblies on wealth and inheritance. And in your own life, talk about what you'll pass on, and why.
E: Any last thought?
S: Money is rarely just money. For most of us it means safety, freedom and looking after the people we love. A fair transition shouldn't take that away from anyone. It should make it reliable for everyone, so nobody has to hoard to feel safe.
