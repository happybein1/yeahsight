S: The state works through laws, taxes and public services. Its strength is that it can reach everyone on equal terms, and enforce rules. Its weakness is that it can be slow and distant. And it's only as good as the politics behind it.
E: And markets?
S: Markets work through prices and exchange. Their strength is coordinating millions of choices at once, and rewarding new ideas. Their weakness is that they serve the people who can pay. And they ignore costs that nobody pays for, like pollution.
E: Then communities.
S: Communities work through trust and shared habits. Associations, cooperatives, religious groups, volunteers, neighbours. The economist Elinor Ostrom showed something important here. Communities can look after shared resources, like forests, fishing grounds or water, and do it well, without the state running them and without anyone owning them privately.
E: That's surprising. What's the catch?
S: Communities can shut out outsiders. And what works in a village is hard to scale up to a whole country.
E: And the fourth, families.
S: Families work through love and obligation. And they provide most of the care in any society. The International Labour Organization estimates that people spend more than sixteen billion hours a day on unpaid care work, worldwide. About three quarters of it is done by women.
E: Sixteen billion hours, every day.
S: Every day. And almost none of it shows up in economic statistics. Yet without it, nothing else would work. No one could go to their job, and no school or hospital could cope.

S: The political system decides who holds power, and how shared choices get made. The legal system turns those choices into binding rules, and settles disputes. The economic system organises production and exchange. Education passes on knowledge, skills and values. Health care prevents illness and restores health. Social protection supports people facing hardship, through pensions, unemployment benefits, disability support and housing. S: Not at all. The sociologist Gøsta Esping-Andersen described three broad types of welfare state. Some leave most things to the market. Some are built around work and the family. And some have the state provide broadly for everyone.
E: So these are separate systems, each doing its own job?

E: And a bad one?
S: Misinformation lowers trust. Lower trust weakens democratic institutions. And weaker institutions do a worse job of protecting the flow of information. So it goes round, and gets worse each time.
E: Who's in charge, then? Politics, or the economy?
S: Power runs both ways. Politics sets the rules for the economy. But economic power, through funding and lobbying, also shapes politics. Neither one simply controls the other.
E: If it's all so tangled, how do you change anything?
S: The systems thinker Donella Meadows had a useful answer. Pushing harder on one part rarely works. It's more effective to change the goals, the rules, or the flow of information, because those shape how all the parts behave.
E: Can you give an example of all this in real life?

E: Another example?
S: The internet and the media. In about twenty years, online platforms changed how news is made, and how it's paid for. That changed politics, trust, and how well people hold together. It's a system that changed slowly for decades, and then very fast.
E: Where do people's own needs come into this?
S: Each player meets different needs. The state and markets mostly cover physical needs and safety. Communities and families also meet the needs for belonging, recognition and care. No public service can fully replace those. So when a society weakens one player, another has to fill the gap.
