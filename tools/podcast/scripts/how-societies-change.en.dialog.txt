S: Both. Change rarely moves at an even pace. You get long calm periods, then short bursts where a lot changes at once.
E: What sets off those bursts?
S: Often a crisis. Wars, depressions, pandemics. After the Second World War, for example, many European countries built their welfare states within just a few years. Ideas that seemed impossible became possible, and then normal.
E: What are the big drivers of change?
S: Three. First, technology. The printing press, the steam engine, electricity, the internet. Each one changed how people work, live and think. Technology rarely decides the outcome on its own, but it changes what's possible.
E: Second?
S: Pressure from outside. Wars, disasters, shortages, competition between countries. They force change, whether people want it or not.
E: And third?
S: Ideas and movements. People organising around a new idea of what's fair. The end of slavery, votes for women, workers' rights, protecting nature. All of them started as minority causes.
E: How does a minority idea become the norm?
S: Political scientists talk about the range of ideas a society considers acceptable at a given time. Movements work by shifting that range, until what was once radical becomes common sense.
E: Is there a tipping point?
S: There's some evidence for one. In a 2018 experiment, the sociologist Damon Centola found that once about a quarter of a group adopted a new norm, the rest tended to follow.
E: A quarter. That's less than I'd have guessed.
S: It's one experiment, but it's a hopeful one. There's also the work of Erica Chenoweth. She found that over the past century, non-violent mass movements succeeded about twice as often as violent ones.
E: Why would peaceful movements do better?
S: Partly because many more people can take part. Her finding that few succeed without broad participation is widely cited, though the exact share is debated.
E: Once a change happens, what makes it last?
S: It has to be built into more than one layer. Laws and institutions, like a pension system or a court. The economy, so people's jobs and savings depend on it. And habits and identity, so people see it as just how things are done.
E: And can it swing back?
S: It can. The economist Karl Polanyi described what he called a double movement. When markets push too far, society pushes back with protections. And the balance shifts again.
E: Any examples of change in our own lifetime?
S: Smoking. Within about fifty years, it went from normal everywhere to banned in most public places in many countries. Research, campaigns, taxes and laws each played a part. Another one is solar power. In about fifteen years, it went from a niche to the cheapest electricity in much of the world.
E: What's the human side of change?
S: Change brings hope, and also fear of losing what we know. People accept change more easily when they feel safe, are treated fairly, and have a say. A change imposed from above, with no voice for those it affects, often triggers a backlash.

S: Big changes look impossible until they happen, and then obvious afterwards. They come from technology, pressure and people, and they last when they're built into rules, the economy and everyday life.
