**In short:** Most of today's financial unfairness was inherited, not chosen. Wealth keeps earning more wealth, and it passes from one generation to the next. Fixing this in one go would cause fear, resistance and damage. We propose a slow, announced path in three phases. First, give everyone a secure floor. Then change how *new* wealth flows: inheritances, land gains, pollution. Finally, let the large inherited fortunes return to a common fund that pays for everyone's start in life. Ordinary savings, pensions and family homes are protected all along the way. The path is started by a group of countries rather than one alone, and every step is chosen to make the economy stronger, not weaker, so that going first doesn't mean falling behind.

## The Question

The other articles in this section describe where we want to go. Basic needs are guaranteed for everyone ([Universal Allowance](/sharing-fairly/universal-allowance/)). Each generation starts from a fairer base ([Inheritance](/sharing-fairly/inheritance/)). And the planet's resources are shared within its limits ([Fair Globe Sharing](/sharing-fairly/fair-globe-sharing/)).

This article asks the harder question: **how do we get there from here**, starting from a world where the richest 10% of people own about three quarters of all wealth and the poorer half owns about 2%?

## Why Change Is Hard

Plans for a fairer society usually fail on the way there, not at the destination. Four things make the transition difficult:

- **Unfairness feeds itself.** Wealth invested in shares, property or land tends to grow faster than wages. Those who already have it get further ahead every year without doing anything, and their children start ahead too.
- **People fear losing what they have.** A family that owns its home, or has saved for retirement, can easily feel threatened by any talk of redistribution, even when it is aimed at fortunes a thousand times larger.
- **Countries compete.** If one country acts alone, large fortunes, companies and investment can move to another one. Even a large group feels the pressure: the European Union, which has gone further than most on fairness and climate, is falling behind the United States and China in income growth.
- **Governments change.** A reform that one government passes, the next can cancel. Long changes need support that lasts beyond one election.

A good transition plan has to answer all four.

## What History Teaches

Large, peaceful shifts in who owns what have happened before:

- **West Germany, 1952.** After the war, the *Lastenausgleich* ("equalisation of burdens") asked people who had kept their property to pay a levy of up to half of its value. They could pay it in small instalments **over 30 years**, mostly out of income. The money went to the millions who had lost everything. Spread out that way, it became bearable, and it helped hold the country together.
- **Japan, 1946 to 1950.** A land reform bought farmland from large landowners and sold it cheaply to the families who worked it. It worked, and rural poverty fell sharply. But landowners were paid in bonds that inflation soon made almost worthless, so many experienced it as confiscation. The lesson: fast and abrupt works, but it leaves bitterness.
- **Alaska and Norway.** Both turned oil income into a common fund instead of letting it go to a few. Alaska pays every resident a yearly dividend from its fund (since 1982). Norway saves its oil money in one of the world's largest funds and only spends about the yearly return. Shared funds like these have lasted for decades, under governments of all colours, because everyone sees what they get from them.
- **Finland, 2017 to 2018.** A two-year test gave 2,000 unemployed people €560 a month with no conditions. They worked slightly more, not less, and felt much more secure and trusting. Testing small first gave real evidence instead of guesses.
- **The global minimum tax, 2021.** More than 130 countries agreed that large companies should pay at least 15% tax wherever they operate. Shared rules like this one and the automatic exchange of bank information show that "money will just leave" can be answered when countries act together.
- **Ireland, 2016 to 2018.** A Citizens' Assembly of 99 people chosen at random studied hard questions for months, and its advice on abortion led to the 2018 referendum. When ordinary people are given the time and facts, they can find agreement on deeply divisive subjects.

## HappyBein's Proposed Direction

*This is a proposal for discussion, not a finished policy. Some points still need further clarification and support.*

### Eight principles

1. **Floor first.** Secure everyone's basic needs before asking anyone to give anything up. People accept change much more easily when they are not afraid.
2. **Announce early, move slowly.** Publish the whole path and its dates many years in advance, so that nobody's plans are destroyed overnight.
3. **Protect the ordinary, focus on the concentrated.** Family homes up to a fair value, pensions, normal savings and working family businesses are protected. The changes apply to very large fortunes.
4. **Make the economy stronger, not weaker.** A fairness plan that weakens the economy won't last. Steps that are fair *and* raise productivity come first, and the pace follows the economic strength of the countries taking part.
5. **Change the flows before the stocks.** Start with new money: inheritances, land values rising without any effort from the owner, pollution and resource use. Only later, and gradually, touch fortunes that already exist.
6. **Make the gains visible.** Money raised goes into a common fund that people can see, such as a starting capital for every young adult and the basic-needs allowance. Not into the general budget, where nobody sees what it pays for.
7. **Start as a group, and tax first what can't move.** Begin with a group of countries large enough to matter, protect it with shared rules at its borders, and start with land, buildings and natural resources, which stay where they are whoever owns them.
8. **Decide together, test first.** Use citizens' assemblies to shape the details, and pilots to check that each step works before it goes national.

### Three phases

**Phase 1: Build the floor and the trust (about the first 5 years)**
- Pilot the [Universal Allowance](/sharing-fairly/universal-allowance/) in a few regions, then extend it step by step.
- Make ownership visible: a public register of who really owns companies, land and large fortunes.
- Tax income from wealth (dividends, rents, capital gains) at the same rates as income from work, and close the main loopholes.
- Start a common fund, at first from a tax on non-renewable fuels and pollution (see [Clean & Safe Energy](/forces/clean-safe-energy/)). Fairness and sustainability are financed by the same move.
- Hold a citizens' assembly on the full path, and publish its timeline.

**Phase 2: Change the flows (roughly years 5 to 20)**
- Introduce a **lifetime cap** on what one person can receive in inheritances and gifts. Anything above the cap goes to the common fund. The cap starts generous and is lowered on a published schedule.
- Gradually shift taxes away from work and onto land values and resource use, which no one created by their own effort.
- The fund starts paying a **starting capital** to every young adult (for example at 18 or 25), for education, a first home or a business.
- Family businesses and farms get a path to pass on the business itself, for example by paying over many years out of its profits, like the German levy.

**Phase 3: Complete the shift (roughly years 20 to 40)**
- Financial inheritance beyond the cap and large donations are phased out, as in the [Inheritance](/sharing-fairly/inheritance/) proposal. Personal and sentimental belongings stay in families.
- The common fund, now large, finances the basic-needs allowance and everyone's starting capital, alongside land and resource income.
- What a person owns then reflects mainly their own life and work, and no longer the luck of being born into a rich family.

## What If One Country Goes First?

Competition between countries is real, and it is the main reason fair plans get watered down. But the answer is not to wait until every country agrees, and certainly not to abolish countries. What's needed is a shared floor of rules, with each country free to go further above it. That is already how trade, the ozone layer and the global minimum tax work. Five things make it possible to go first without losing:

1. **Tax what can't leave first.** Land, buildings and natural resources stay in the country, whoever owns them. That is why the plan starts there, and only later moves on to fortunes that can travel.
2. **Make leaving costly.** Germany, France and Norway already tax the gains of rich people who move abroad. The United States taxes its citizens wherever they live.
3. **Protect the borders without closing them.** The EU's carbon border charge, from 2026, makes imports from countries without a carbon price pay the difference, so producers who go first aren't undercut. The same idea can apply to other shared rules.
4. **Start as a group, not alone.** A group the size of the EU is too large a market to walk away from. Others then join over time, as happened with the global minimum tax.
5. **Choose steps that make the economy stronger.** Europe's slower growth comes mainly from expensive energy, too little investment in new technology and markets split into 27 sets of rules, not from fairness itself. Several steps in this plan address those directly:
   - Moving taxes off work and onto land and resources makes work cheaper for businesses. Economists widely see a land tax as the least harmful tax there is.
   - Cheap renewable energy (roadmap step 3) goes straight at Europe's biggest cost problem.
   - A starting capital for every young adult means more people able to study, start a business or take a risk, not only the children of the rich.
   - The common fund can invest, as Norway's does, with a small, capped part for new technology at home (see "Who Manages the Fund?" below).

Countries also gain from fairness in ways growth figures don't show. Denmark, Sweden and the Netherlands combine high taxes with strong economies, partly because security, good education and trust attract people and businesses too. And richer countries going first is itself part of fairness between countries (see [Fair Globe Sharing](/sharing-fairly/fair-globe-sharing/)).

## Who Manages the Fund?

The common fund will become very large, so it has to be protected from waste and from political favours. No single party should control it. The job is split four ways:

1. **Parliament writes the rules once, in a law that is hard to change.** The law sets what the fund pays for (the basic-needs allowance and every young adult's starting capital), how much it may pay out each year and what it may invest in. It doesn't decide year by year where the money goes.
2. **An independent public body manages the money day to day.** Professional managers with long, fixed terms invest it at arm's length from the government, the way a central bank sets interest rates. Norway's oil fund is run this way, by a unit of its central bank, not by ministers.
3. **Citizens watch over it.** A small panel of citizens chosen at random, renewed regularly, reviews the fund every year and reports publicly. The public auditor checks the accounts, and anyone can see online what came in and what went out.
4. **Payouts follow a fixed formula, not a yearly political choice.** For example, the fund pays out at most about 3% of its value each year, shared equally. Norway uses a similar rule.

Both the fund and its payout formula need the strongest protection available, such as the constitution, or a large majority or a referendum to change them. Alaska shows why: its fund is protected in the state constitution, but the yearly dividend formula was only an ordinary law. Since 2016, short of money, the state has repeatedly paid out less than the formula promised, and trust has suffered.

Most of the fund stays in broad, low-risk investments spread across many companies and countries. Investing at home is where political favours creep in ("put the money into my region's factory"), which is why Norway's fund invests only abroad. So only a small, capped part goes to new technology at home, under its own published rules.

### Is the fund shared between countries?

Not at first. Each country keeps its own fund, filled by its own taxes on inheritances, land and pollution, and answering to its own parliament and citizens. People accept paying more when they can see it coming back to them and their neighbours. And countries start at very different levels of wealth: a single fund from day one would mean large transfers between them, the fastest way to lose voters on both sides.

What the group shares from the start are the **rules**: the same inheritance cap rules, ownership registers and payout principles. That way nobody gains by moving a fortune from one member country to another.

On top, a **small shared fund** handles what crosses borders. It is paid for by money the group raises together, mainly the carbon border charge (the European Commission has already proposed that most of that revenue go to the EU's shared budget). It helps poorer members reach the common floor faster and pays for shared investments such as power grids between countries. There is a precedent: in 2020 the EU borrowed together, at large scale, for the first time, about €750 billion for the recovery after Covid.

As trust grows and the gaps between countries shrink, more can be pooled. People who move between member countries keep their rights: the allowance follows where you live, and your starting capital is yours wherever you go. Sharing beyond the group is a further step (see [Fair Globe Sharing](/sharing-fairly/fair-globe-sharing/)).

## Objections

- **"It's my money, and I want to give it to my children."** The wish to protect one's children is deeply human. That is why the plan starts by guaranteeing every child a floor and a starting capital. The security parents want for their own children becomes something all children get.
- **"The rich will leave."** Some will try. The answer is a group of countries acting together, taxes on leaving, ownership registers, and taxes tied to land and activity *inside* the country, which can't be moved abroad (see "What If One Country Goes First?" above).
- **"We'll lose against countries that don't do this."** Only if the steps make the economy weaker. That's why steps that also raise productivity come first, and why the pace follows the economy's strength.
- **"Governments will waste the money."** That is why the fund is separate, transparent, with clear rules on what it pays for, and visible to every citizen, like Norway's.
- **"The next government will cancel it."** A path decided with citizens, announced long in advance and paying benefits everyone can see is much harder to cancel. Few politicians would dare to take away every young adult's starting capital.
- **"Forty years is too slow."** Slow at the top, but not at the bottom: the floor comes first, in the first years. The slowness is in the part that, done fast, would cause fear and flight.

## First Steps

Things that can start now, without waiting for the whole plan:

- Support transparency: public registers of who really owns companies and land.
- Support taxing income from wealth and income from work in the same way.
- Back pilots of guaranteed basic needs where you live.
- Ask for citizens' assemblies on wealth and inheritance.
- In your own life: talk about what you will pass on and why, and consider giving to people or causes beyond your own family.

## The Human Side

Money is rarely just money. For most people it stands for safety, freedom and the ability to look after those they love. These are needs every person has, described in YeahCore as [physical health and energy, safety and security, social connection, autonomy, and meaning and purpose](https://yeahcore.happybein.com/decision-foundation/find-your-directional-purpose/humans-physiological-needs/). A fair transition shouldn't take these away from anyone. It should make them reliable for everyone, so that nobody has to hoard to feel safe.

## Open Questions

- Where exactly should the lifetime cap start, and how fast should it fall?
- What counts as a "fair value" family home that stays fully protected?
- How can countries at different levels of wealth move along this path together?
- How can the past be acknowledged, for example wealth built on colonial extraction, without making the transition impossible?
